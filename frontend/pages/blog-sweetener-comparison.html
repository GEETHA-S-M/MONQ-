Monk Fruit vs. Stevia vs. Erythritol: How They Actually Compare

By the monq. team

Back to the Journal

The sweetener aisle, decoded

Monk fruit, stevia, erythritol, allulose — they all show up in the same “sugar-free” conversation, but they’re chemically nothing alike, and they don’t behave the same way in your cup or your body. Here’s how the most common ones actually stack up.

Monk fruit

A fruit extract, sweetened by mogrosides. Zero calories, glycemic index of zero, and a clean taste with no bitterness at typical concentrations. The catch: most commercial monk fruit products are blended with a bulking agent, so read the ingredient list, not just the front of the pack.

Stevia

A leaf extract, sweetened by steviol glycosides. Also zero-calorie and zero-glycemic-impact, but many people detect a distinct bitter or liquorice-like aftertaste, especially at higher concentrations — it’s the most common complaint in head-to-head taste tests.

Erythritol

A sugar alcohol, usually produced by fermenting corn starch. It has a clean taste and about 6% of sugar’s calories, but in larger amounts it can cause digestive discomfort — bloating or a laxative effect — in sensitive people, since sugar alcohols aren’t fully absorbed in the small intestine.

Allulose

A rare sugar found naturally in small amounts in figs and raisins. It tastes very close to real sugar and browns similarly in baking, with about 10% of sugar’s calories and minimal glycemic impact — but it’s roughly 70% as sweet as sugar, so recipes need slightly more of it by volume.

Where monq. lands

monq. is monk fruit extract on its own — no erythritol for digestive sensitivity to worry about, no stevia bitterness to mask, and no need to double the quantity the way you would with allulose. The trade-off is the one every monk fruit product shares: it won’t caramelise quite like sugar or allulose in high-heat baking.
